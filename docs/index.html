Search APIs, graded on their sources

Benchmarks of search APIs grade the answer a model writes afterwards, a number that is mostly a score of the model. With Sourcery I wanted to control for the LLM and judge purely on the fetched results. All eight providers got the same 204 questions in a single run, then 3 models — Claude Sonnet 5, GPT-5.6 and GLM 5.2 — read every page and scored it on a 0–3 scale. The method has more on this.

Why it is built this way

Some of what follows is a principled choice and some of it is my budget. They are worth telling apart, so here is which is which.

Pooling was a cost decision first. Every page is graded once and that rating counts for every provider that returned it. There is a real methodological argument for it, and I would make it honestly: a page is detached from whoever returned it before anyone grades it, so there is no provider name for a judge to be swayed by. But I picked it because the eight arms returned 12,954 question-page pairs that collapse to 7,496 unique ones, and grading the unique set meant 42% fewer judge calls. I could not have afforded the run otherwise. What it costs is real and I would rather you hear it from me: where several arms returned the same URL, the longest extraction anyone returned is the one that got graded, so extraction quality is averaged away on those pages.

The 1600-character cut on each source is the same story. It is a realistic slice of an agent's context budget, which is true, and it is also the cheapest way to make 22,457 ratings affordable. A bigger budget would have cost more and might have reordered the bottom of the table.

Three judges, not five. Five models passed calibration against human-settled pairs and none of them rated a keyword-stuffed page above zero. I ran three of them, because three was what I could pay for. And this is one run on one set of questions: nothing here is measured twice, so no number on this page carries an estimate of how much it would move if I ran it again.

Results

Each provider gave back eight pages per question, and a judge saw all eight together and answered one question: could you answer from these alone? Zero means nothing useful, three means a full answer. That is the set rating, and it is the headline on this page: it grades the eight results as a set, not one page at a time.

Perplexity is clear of everyone else. Below it there are two groups, and inside either one the order doesn't mean anything. Ask again next week and they could come back in a different sequence. The gap between the two groups is the actual result: Exa to Tavily is four tenths of a point on the 0–3 scale, bigger than anything inside either cluster.

The ordering holds without the 0–3 scale. Perplexity returns a page that fully answers the question 49.9% of the time, Bright Data manages 14.9%, and the ranking comes back the same, so none of this rests on the rubric being right.

Base vs. hard questions

I wrote half the set to be hard: multi-hop, ambiguous, or needing a source that isn't the first result.

This chart is the page rating, not the set rating above: it scores each page on its own rather than the eight together. That is why the order is different: Exa's individual pages beat Parallel's, while Parallel's eight fit together better and win on the set.

Benchmark search on questions your model could nearly answer on its own and every provider looks fine. The difference only shows up on the ones you actually needed help with.

Cost

The three providers on the cheapest tier take three of the top four places; the outright cheapest places sixth. Serper matches Tavily at a fifty-fourth of the cost. It returns links and snippets and never page content, so if you already run a scraper, that is the part you were paying extra for.

Speed

Speed is the other thing the price does not buy. Brave answers in a median of 0.6s per search; Serper, the cheapest arm, takes 3.2s. Bright Data is the slowest at 34s, about 59 times the fastest, and its slowest one search in ten takes 75s or worse.

Returned content

Brave returns real page text on 1% of results, which is why the picking section lists it as summaries-only. Exa keeps headings and drops links. Bright Data keeps nearly every image and no tables.

The other axis is volume: an agent gives each source a slice of its context, and this run allows 1600 characters, which is a realistic budget. 98% of Firecrawl's pages run past it; 40% of Perplexity's do. Firecrawl returns whole documents and Perplexity returns excerpts, and past the budget that difference stops reaching the model.

Why this disagrees with answer-graded indexes

Artificial Analysis published a search index the day before this went out. It ranks Firecrawl third and this run puts it seventh, because the two grade different things. They grade what an agent eventually produces: up to 25 turns and roughly nine to thirteen searches per task, and then the final answer gets scored, so a weak first result set just gets repaired by searching again. Sourcery grades what one search hands back: eight results, one shot, no recovery. Their questions are static obscure facts, mine lean fresh.

Neither index rewards Firecrawl's extraction, for opposite reasons. Their settings disable its scraping by name and route everyone through one generic extractor. This run asked for full page markdown, got it, and then metered every source to the same context budget, which most of those pages exceed.

Their index carries no confidence intervals and their top five span two points. On their published sample sizes that gap is inside the noise, which is the same reason three providers here are drawn as a tie.

Notes from the run

Getting these numbers was messier than the table makes it look, and the parts that went wrong say as much about these products as the ranking does.

Bright Data throttles above one concurrent request, so its arm ran at a crawl and needed a second repair pass that went past midnight, which is why this run is stamped across two days instead of one. Tavily's plan ran out around question 150 and the rest of its questions had to wait. Firecrawl went through 3,493 credits in a day, 70% of a month's allowance and $25 of prepaid credit, because it bills web and news results separately and only the web half ends up as sources; the news half buys publish dates I never used. I also tried a keyless arm as a free-floor control and dropped it after eight consecutive captcha failures and no successes at all.

One thing I would tell anyone repeating this: run it before you read it. A batching bug cost about $19 of retrieval that produced nothing usable, and no amount of staring at the code had turned it up; it only showed itself when the requests actually went out. Of 204 questions across eight providers, 1,631 of 1,632 fetches came back clean; the single miss is Firecrawl timing out at 300 seconds, which I checked against their API by hand before recording it. The whole thing cost $40.08, and what capped it was rate limits rather than the bill.

Six providers, six jobs

What you're building decides this more than the ranking does. Three of these eight are tied on the headline and they still aren't interchangeable: one returns whole documents, one returns snippets, one returns prose with the links stripped out. So pick on the job. And weigh what your code can check: it can see an empty result and handle it; it cannot see a confident near-miss.

Perplexitybest overall

Clear of the field and on the cheapest tier. Fully answers 49.9% of the time, about 1.4× anyone else. Returns excerpts rather than whole pages, and it is the easiest of the eight to fool: asked for the default of max_parallel_vacuum_workers, a Postgres setting that does not exist, it came back with a page documenting max_parallel_maintenance_workers, a real setting with a real default, and two of the three judges read that as a direct answer.

Serperbest raw search

Links and snippets, no page content, $0.001 a query. Matches Tavily's score at a fifty-fourth of the price. The right answer if you already have a scraper and only need the index.

Tavilybest for full documents

Full page markdown on 98% of results, with headings, links and images intact. Outscores Firecrawl and costs less. If your agent needs the document rather than a summary of it, start here.

Exabest for agents

Keeps structure and strips navigation: 89% of its pages have headings, 7% have links. Top-cluster relevance. Good when you are feeding text to a model and link noise costs you context.

Firecrawlhardest to fool

On the 12 questions with no answer, it produced the fewest convincing near-misses, beating Perplexity on all 12. That has to be read next to its seventh place overall, though, because it returns fewer good pages than the others and so has fewer chances to return a convincing wrong one. Most expensive of the eight.

Bravesummaries only

Ranks second on relevance and returns real page text on 1% of results, and its content is search snippets joined together. Fine for grounding a summary, wrong for anything that has to read a page.
