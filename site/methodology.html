Methodology

The questions

__N_QUESTIONS__ questions, written rather than collected: __N_BASE__ ordinary, __N_HARD__ deliberately hard, __N_UNANS__ with no answer anywhere. I wrote all of them. That is the sample's biggest weakness and there is no way around saying so — whatever I found interesting is in there and whatever I did not think to ask is not. All three sets are frozen and public, so you can read them and judge the resemblance to your own traffic yourself.

They spread across six kinds of task — breaking news, how-to, product lookup, local and geographic, recent releases, and live numbers — and six subject areas, weighted toward software and everyday questions because that is what I actually point a retrieval agent at. Every question carries a note written before the run saying what a correct page contains and what a plausible wrong one looks like. The judges never see those notes. They exist so that when a rating looks wrong in the explorer, there is a written intent to argue against rather than a memory.

What makes the hard half hard

Four things, usually two at a time. Trap: a plausible wrong answer sits close to the right one, an older figure or a superseded draft. Buried: the answer exists but not on the first result. Multi: no single page has all of it. Obscure: the subject is thinly covered, so the index either has it or does not.

The point of the split is that easy questions make providers look alike. A question your model could nearly answer unaided does not test retrieval much, and if a whole benchmark is built from those, every provider passes.

Why __N_UNANS__ questions with no answer

Each of these asks about something that sounds entirely real and does not exist: a Postgres setting called max_parallel_vacuum_workers, a grace_period option in a Docker Compose healthcheck, a statistic nobody publishes. Search them and you get authoritative, tightly on-topic pages about the neighbouring real thing.

Correct behaviour is that every page rates 0 or 1. A page rated 2 or 3 means either a provider surfaced something that invented the answer or a judge invented the relevance, and the explorer shows which. This is the one part of the set where a higher score is a worse result, and it is the only place the run can see a confident near-miss at all — the failure your own code cannot check for, because a near-miss arrives looking exactly like a hit.

What run 2 changed, and why

Run 1 asked 48 questions of four providers and graded the answer a model wrote from what came back. Then it checked whether source quality and answer quality moved together, and they barely did: a correlation of 0.16, where 1 is lockstep and 0 is unrelated. The model was carrying most of the result. That is the finding this whole run is a reaction to, and it is why nothing here grades an answer.

So run 2 grades the sources and never writes an answer, doubles the field to eight providers, and uses three judges from three different labs instead of one, so that no single model's taste is the ranking. It also gives the slow arms room to finish rather than timing them out — looking at you, Bright Data — because a provider that is merely slow should show up as slow in a latency column, not as missing data in a quality one.

One thing changed mid-run and is worth knowing about. The set prompt replaced an earlier one that graded whether sources looked good: fresh, authoritative, cleanly extracted. On a question with no answer anywhere it returned a passing score while the page judge was correctly rating every page 0 or 1. The sources were good. They just could not answer. Everything published here was scored with the replacement.

The two ratings

Two separate questions, asked at two scopes, on the same 0–3 scale. Every score comes with a written definition of what it means, because two models can agree on what a 2 means while a bare number on a wide scale is something each one invents its own version of.

They are separate judge calls, and the set rating is not an average of page ratings. That matters enough to say plainly: a per-page average cannot answer the set question. __NUM_SOURCES__ pages each holding a third of the answer and __NUM_SOURCES__ pages holding nothing average out to something similar, and only one of those sets lets you answer.

Each judge scores independently and its score is never merged with another judge's before it is recorded — every individual verdict is in the explorer with the sentence that judge wrote to justify it. The averaging happens once, at the end, and only for display: a provider's set rating on the results page is the mean of its per-question scores across all __N_QUESTIONS__ questions and all three judges.

What varies

One question goes to all eight providers. Each returns up to __NUM_SOURCES__ results. Nothing else differs between arms: same prompt, same judges, same number of results requested, caching off. Of the eight arms, __ARMS_DAY1__ finished inside a single day. Tavily and Bright Data ran into their own rate limits and finished in the early hours of the next morning, and that is the one respect in which the arms were not treated identically: a page that changed overnight would have been seen differently by those two.

The settings live in one file, core/controls.ts, and every stage imports from it, so there is only ever one value of anything for a stage to read. Every provider was set to the highest-quality tier it offers; where a depth or content knob existed it was turned up.

Serper is the one deliberate downgrade: it sells a separate scrape endpoint and this run does not call it, and that choice was pre-registered. Bright Data is the only provider needing a second paid product to get page text, and its extraction step missed roughly half the URLs it tried.

Pooling

Providers overlap heavily. Every page from every arm is collapsed into one pool keyed by normalized URL and graded once; that rating counts for each provider that returned it. __OVERLAP_ONE__% of the __UNIQUE_PAIRS__ pages came from exactly one provider and __OVERLAP_ALL__ came from all eight.

Pooling blinds the judge structurally: a page is detached from whoever returned it before grading, so there is no provider name to be swayed by. It was a cost decision first, though, and the cost is real — where several arms returned the same URL, the longest extraction anyone returned is what gets graded, so extraction quality is averaged away on those pages. With a bigger budget I would not pool.

The statistics, in words

Confidence interval

Where the real value probably sits, given that __N_QUESTIONS__ questions is a sample. Two overlapping intervals mean the order between those providers is not a finding.

Variance shares

How much of everything that varies is explained by each thing that could explain it. The provider explains __VAR_PROVIDER__%; which judge you picked explains __VAR_JUDGE__%.

Kappa

Agreement between two judges after subtracting the agreement you would get by chance. These three run __KAPPA_LO__ to __KAPPA_HI__: they agree on the order and argue about the decimals.

Sign test

Comparing two providers across the same small set of questions by counting wins, then asking how often a split that lopsided would happen by luck. Used on the __N_UNANS__ unanswerable questions, where eight providers cannot be ranked but two can be compared.

What this cannot tell you

Who extracts a page best

Each source gets a __TRUNC_LIMIT__-character slice, which is what an agent with a real context budget would allow. A larger slice would let the providers that return whole documents show more of what they extract, and it could reorder the bottom of the table. Pooling costs the same measurement a second way: on the __OVERLAP_MULTI_PCT__% of pages that several arms returned, the longest extraction anyone returned is the one that got graded. Measuring extraction properly means holding the URLs fixed and varying only the extractor, and that run has not happened.

What your agent will answer

Nothing here grades an answer, on purpose. An answer-graded benchmark measures that, bearing in mind such a number also grades your model.

How any of this behaves at scale

Every arm was run once, at whatever concurrency it tolerated, so nothing here measures throughput, quota behaviour, or what any of these costs at volume. What this run did collide with is written up in notes from the run: a provider that throttles at one request, a plan that ran out mid-way, a day that ate most of a month's credits. Those are things that happened once; none of them is a measurement.

Whether the questions look like yours

They are written, not collected from real traffic, and I wrote all of them. The harness exists so you can run your own instead, which is the only real answer to this one.

Whether tomorrow would come out the same

This is one run, fetched __FETCHED__. Every provider is a moving target: indexes change, tiers change, and a model behind one of these can be swapped without telling you. Nothing here is measured twice, so none of these numbers carries an estimate of how much it would move on a re-run. The confidence intervals describe sampling across questions within this one run, which is a narrower claim than it looks.

Whether the judges are right

Three large language models grade every page, and they agree with each other well enough to trust the ranking, kappa __KAPPA_LO__ to __KAPPA_HI__, moderate on a four-point scale. That is agreement among themselves, not agreement with a person. They were calibrated against human-settled pairs before the run, but three models trained on overlapping text can share a blind spot and agree anyway, and the __N_UNANS__ unanswerable questions show one: a plausible near-miss fools some of them.

How fast any of them are for you

The table has a median latency column, so timing is measured, but it is measured from one machine on one network over two days, and a provider that is quick from my connection may not be quick from your region. Take the ordering seriously and the absolute numbers loosely.
